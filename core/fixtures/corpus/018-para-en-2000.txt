There is a particular kind of tiredness that comes from making small decisions all day, and I did not recognise it as tiredness for years. It does not feel like effort; it feels like fog. You sit down to do the one thing that matters and find that you have, without noticing, spent the first hour answering questions that did not need answering: which of three nearly identical tools to use, whether a message deserves a reply now or later, what to name a file. Each of these costs almost nothing, which is exactly why they are dangerous. A cost that is almost nothing is a cost you never budget for, and you pay it two hundred times a day. The trick I eventually found is embarrassingly simple: remove the decision rather than speed it up. I keep one text editor and refuse to evaluate others. Messages are read twice a day, at fixed times, and the app is closed in between. Files get a date and the first three words of their content, chosen by a script rather than by me. None of this is new; every productivity book says something like it. What took me years to accept is that the value is not in the minutes saved but in the attention preserved, and attention does not add up linearly. Ten interruptions of one minute each do not cost ten minutes; they cost the deep hour that never started. I notice this most clearly on the days when the system fails, when a tool update forces a choice or a message arrives through a channel I forgot to close. The fog comes back within the hour, and with it the strange conviction that I have been busy. I have not been busy. I have been deciding. The difference matters because busy people finish things, and deciding people only feel like they might. So the rule now is that anything I decide more than once a week gets automated, defaulted, or deleted, and the rest of the week is spent on the small number of decisions that nobody else can make for me. It is not a heroic system. It is a fence around an hour, and the hour is where the work happens.
